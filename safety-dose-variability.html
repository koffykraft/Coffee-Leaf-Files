Door Nine - Safety

Safety, Dose and Variability

Why the finished cup matters more than the plant name, what caffeine and EGCG are doing in the safety discussion, and where processing changes the question.

ExposureCaffeineEGCGEU referenceProcessingMeaning

Safety depends on exposure. The same coffee species can produce a weak infusion, a strong infusion, a concentrated extract or a whole-leaf food. Those are not the same dose.

The practical safety question

What is in this finished product, how much is in one serving, and how often is it consumed?

That question is more useful than asking whether "coffee leaf" is safe in the abstract.

Caffeine: the main familiar variable

Caffeine in coffee leaves varies widely. The 2022 safety review notes that leaf age, climate, species, variety and processing can all change the amount. In samples discussed in that review, dry-leaf caffeine varied substantially, and the authors treated caffeine as the main familiar safety consideration for coffee leaf tea.

The review compared coffee leaf tea with other caffeinated drinks and concluded that, apart from the usual caffeine concern common to coffee-related beverages, the available evidence did not suggest a special consumer risk from the beverage itself.

Why this matters in practice

A producer should not copy a caffeine value from another farm or paper. Young leaves and some varieties may carry more caffeine than older leaves. Processing and brew strength can change what reaches the cup.

Do not turn an average into a promise

A low value measured in one coffee leaf drink does not prove that every coffee leaf product is low in caffeine. The finished beverage should be measured if caffeine level is important to positioning or regular use.

Source: Tritsch et al., Foods, 2022.

EGCG: why it appears in the safety literature

EGCG is a catechin widely discussed in tea safety because very high supplemental exposures have been associated with liver risk. The 2022 coffee leaf review therefore examined EGCG alongside caffeine.

In the coffee leaf material reviewed, EGCG was generally absent or below the exposure level that raised toxicological concern in the cited EFSA work. The authors therefore regarded EGCG as less important than caffeine for ordinary coffee leaf tea safety.

Plain meaning

The safety issue is not that coffee leaf tea is known to contain dangerous EGCG levels. Rather, regulators checked a compound that is already important in tea safety and found it comparatively uncritical in the reviewed coffee leaf context.

What the European rules tell us

The European Union authorised coffee leaf infusion as a traditional food from a third country in 2020 and set specifications for the finished infusion. Later regulation expanded authorised uses.

This is useful because it shifts attention from the botanical name to a defined food product. The regulation addresses the infusion that a consumer receives, not an unlimited range of extracts or experimental preparations.

How to use the EU reference

Treat it as a benchmark for a defined coffee leaf food, not as a universal recipe for every product or country. Local food law and actual product testing still matter.

  • Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/917Coffee leaf infusion authorisation and specifications.
  • Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/931Extension of authorised uses.

Why processing creates new safety questions

Fermentation

Fermentation adds biological activity before the drink is made. Moisture, temperature, duration, sanitation, inoculated cultures and unwanted microbial growth need control. A successful sensory fermentation is not automatically a microbiologically safe process.

Smoking and roasting

Heat and smoke create new aroma chemistry. That makes process control and finished-product testing more relevant than simply assuming that a traditional smoke source is harmless.

Whole-leaf foods

An infusion leaves much of the insoluble plant material behind. Eating leaf powder or cooked leaf changes exposure because the consumer receives material that would normally remain in the spent leaf.

Agricultural source

Pesticide residues, heavy metals, dust, microbial load and handling contamination depend on the farm and process. These are ordinary food-safety questions and should not be overshadowed by discussion of interesting phytochemicals.

A simple safety framework

1

Define the material

Species, cultivar, leaf age, source and agricultural history.

2

Define the process

Withering, fermentation, roasting, smoking, drying and storage.

3

Define the serving

Leaf dose, water volume, brew time, temperature and serving size.

4

Measure what matters

Caffeine and relevant chemistry, plus ordinary food-safety testing appropriate to the product.

5

Do not borrow safety from another product

A result for one cultivar, extract or brew strength should not be silently transferred to another.
