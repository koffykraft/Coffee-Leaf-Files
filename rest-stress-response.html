Door Nine - Rest and stress

Rest, Stress and Human Response

How a drink can affect experience, what cortisol can and cannot tell us, and what the 2025 human study actually adds.

ExperienceCortisol2025 studyWhat may contributeMeaning

A person can call a drink calming, refreshing or restorative for many reasons. Taste and aroma matter. Temperature matters. Caffeine matters. The setting and expectation matter. Biology may matter too. A good human study tries to separate at least some of those influences.

Door Six already covers sensory experience. Here the question is narrower: did researchers measure anything in people beyond liking the drink?

What is cortisol?

Cortisol is a hormone involved in the body's response to challenge and in normal daily regulation. It is not simply a "stress chemical." Levels normally rise and fall across the day and can be affected by sleep, food, time of sampling, anticipation, illness and many other factors.

Why researchers measure saliva

Salivary cortisol gives a convenient, non-invasive snapshot of cortisol at a particular time. A change can be interesting, but one measurement does not by itself tell us that a person is less stressed in daily life.

What the 2025 coffee leaf study did

Human intervention study, 2025

Researchers prepared green-, black- and oolong-style coffee leaf teas and optimized brewing conditions within roughly 74 to 81 degrees C and 4 to 12 minutes. They measured total phenolics, GABA and other compounds, and they also assessed sensory and emotional responses in participants.

The oolong-style preparation had the highest reported total phenolic content in the study at 35.61 mg GAE/g and contained about 0.40 mg/g GABA. It was also described as relatively sweet, low in bitterness and strongly preferred.

After the oolong-style preparation, the researchers reported a reduction in salivary cortisol and favourable emotional responses.

Why this is more useful than a test-tube result

People actually consumed a defined coffee leaf preparation, and the study included both subjective responses and a physiological measure. That moves the evidence one step closer to the real drinking experience.

What one study cannot settle

We still do not know how reproducible the cortisol change is, how long it lasts, whether it is caused by GABA, phenolics, sensory pleasure, caffeine level, expectation or some combination, or whether it translates into a meaningful change in everyday stress.

Source: Fibrianto et al., Applied Food Research, 2025.

What may be contributing to the response?

GABA

GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the human nervous system, and it can also be measured in foods. Finding GABA in a beverage does not mean dietary GABA automatically reaches the brain in the same way as GABA made inside the nervous system.

Phenolics

The oolong-style tea also had high total phenolics in this study. Phenolic content may matter biologically, but total phenolics is a broad measure rather than proof of a particular human mechanism.

Sensory pleasure

A pleasant, low-bitterness drink can change mood and expectation simply because the experience is enjoyable. That is not a nuisance variable; it is part of real beverage use, but it needs to be separated from pharmacological claims.

Caffeine and ritual

Caffeine level, warmth or coolness, the act of pausing, and the social setting can all influence how a drink feels. Future studies need controls that make these influences easier to distinguish.

What can we reasonably say today?

A fair summary

There is an early human signal that processing style may influence both the sensory experience and a stress-related physiological measure. That is enough to justify replication. It is not enough to describe coffee leaf tea as a treatment for stress or anxiety, or as equivalent to a medicine or supplement.
