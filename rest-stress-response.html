Door Nine - Rest and stress

Rest, Stress and Human Response

One early human study gives us something worth following, but not something to overstate.

The questionThe studyCortisolMeaningSource

People can describe a drink as calming, refreshing or restorative for many reasons. Taste, aroma, ritual, temperature, caffeine, expectation and biology may all contribute. A human study can begin to separate some of these possibilities, but one study cannot settle them all.

The sensory side is already covered in Door Six. See The Buna Sensory School and The Chemistry. This page focuses only on the human-response evidence.

What did the 2025 study ask?

Human intervention, 2025

Researchers compared green-, black- and oolong-style coffee leaf teas. They looked at the chemistry of the preparations, how participants experienced them, and physiological measures including salivary cortisol.

The oolong-style preparation had the highest reported total phenolic content in that study and about 0.40 mg/g GABA. The researchers also reported favourable emotional responses and a reduction in salivary cortisol after the oolong-style preparation.

Source: Fibrianto et al., Applied Food Research, 2025.

What does a cortisol result mean?

Cortisol is a hormone involved in the body's stress response. It also changes naturally during the day and can be affected by many things. A change in salivary cortisol is interesting, but it does not automatically mean that a drink is a treatment for stress or anxiety.

Why the result is worth noting

Human participants consumed a defined preparation and a physiological marker changed. That gives a clear reason to repeat the work under stronger controls.

Why caution is still needed

The finding needs larger controlled studies before we know how reliable it is, how long it lasts and whether it matters in daily life.

What can we reasonably say?

There is an early human signal that some coffee leaf preparations may influence stress-related measures and subjective experience. It does not establish a medical treatment, and it does not show equivalence to a medicine or supplement.
